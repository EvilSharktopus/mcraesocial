Solo Part 1 — You

Before any theory.
Just you.

We're going to spend this unit stress-testing ideas about personal identity — what makes a person who they are, what survives change, what gets lost. Before we get into any of that, we start with the most direct version of the question:

What makes you you?

Answer the questions below from gut instinct only. No theory yet. No right answers. Just your honest first reaction.

1
List 3–5 things that make you who you are. Not facts about your life — the core of you. What would disappear if you subtracted it?
2
If you woke up tomorrow with no memories at all — a completely blank slate — would that person still be you? Why or why not?
3
If your body were somehow replaced but all your memories stayed perfectly intact — same thoughts, same feelings, same personality — would that still be you?
4
What is the one thing about you that, if removed, would make you a completely different person?

Hold onto your answers.

You'll come back to them at the end. By then you might agree with yourself — or you might not. Either is the point.

Solo → Group Part 2 — The Prestige

The copy problem.

The setup

Angier's machine doesn't transport him. It copies him — perfectly. Every night a new Angier walks out of the machine across the stage while the original falls through a trapdoor into a tank of water and drowns. Both are completely real. Both have the same memories, the same feelings, the same everything. Angier knew this. He did it hundreds of times.

2.1 Solo

1
Which Angier is the real one — the one who walks out, or the one who drowns? Or is there no right answer?
2
The one who drowns chose to get in the machine. He knew what would happen. Is his death a murder, a suicide, or something else entirely?
3
Now run yourself through the machine. A perfect copy of you steps out the other side — same memories, same personality, same everything. Is that still you? What's different, if anything?

2.2 Three theories — quick read

Here are three ways philosophers have answered the identity question. Read them now — don't overthink, just absorb.

Body theory

You are your physical body. Same body = same person. If the body is different, it's a different person — regardless of memories.

Memory theory

You are your memories and psychological continuity. Whoever remembers being you, is you. The body is just the container.

Soul theory

You are something non-physical — a soul or essence — that can't be copied, scanned, or destroyed. The machine can't touch it.

Solo → Group Part 3 — Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind

The erasure problem.

The setup

Joel and Clementine erase each other — completely — from their memories. The procedure is real, clinical, and permanent. After it's done, they meet again as strangers. They fall for each other again. They don't remember why they broke up. They don't remember what it cost them.

Crucially: Joel changes his mind midway through. He tries to hold on. He can't.

3.1 Solo

1
If Joel remembers nothing about Clementine, is he still the same Joel who loved her? Or did erasing her erase part of him?
2
Joel tries to cling to the memories even as they're being deleted. He clearly thinks losing them means losing something important. Is he right? What exactly would he be losing?
3
The Prestige asked about copying. This film asks about erasing. Which one feels like a bigger threat to your identity — and why?
4
Think about an experience that genuinely shaped who you are — something that changed how you see yourself or the world. If that memory were erased, do you think you'd still make the same kinds of choices? Would people who know you still recognize you?

3.2 A wrinkle — read this

Memory theory has a problem.

If you are your memories, then erasing them destroys you. But Joel clearly survives the procedure — he wakes up the next morning, he goes to work, he feels things. There's still a Joel. So either memory theory is wrong, or something about identity goes deeper than memory.

And: Joel and Clementine fall for each other again. If their identities are built from memory, and those memories are gone — why do they end up in the same place? Is there something underneath memory that persists?

3.3 Group discussion

One person in the group records the answers below. Everyone else: write that person's name on your sheet instead.
5
Joel chose to erase Clementine. Then he tried to stop it. Which choice was more him — the one who started the procedure, or the one who tried to hold on? Can both be him?
6
If everything you remembered was wiped clean, what do you think someone who knows you well would still recognize about you? What would be left that isn't just memory?
Solo → Group Part 4 — Arrival

The time problem.

The setup

Louise learns the alien language and gains something the aliens have: the ability to perceive time non-linearly. She sees her future — including her daughter, who will die young. She sees herself making choices she hasn't made yet.

Knowing all of this, she chooses to have her daughter anyway.

4.1 Solo

1
If Louise already knows everything that will happen — her daughter, the grief, all of it — does she have free will? Or is she just going through motions she's already seen?
2
The Prestige and Eternal Sunshine both assume time moves forward — there's a "before" and "after" to identity. Arrival breaks that. If past, present, and future all exist simultaneously, what happens to the idea of a self that persists over time?
3
Louise chooses to have her daughter knowing the pain it will cause. Does the choice mean more or less because she knew the outcome in advance?
4
Think about a choice you've made that you can't undo — something that set your life on a particular path. If you had known exactly how it would turn out, do you think you would have made the same choice? Does knowing the outcome change whether it was really a choice at all?

4.2 The block universe — read this

A note on time

Some physicists and philosophers believe in what's called the block universe: the idea that past, present, and future all equally exist — time doesn't "flow," we just experience it that way. On this view, there's no meaningful difference between "who you were" and "who you will be." They all exist, simultaneously, in one four-dimensional structure.

If that's true: is there even a "you" that persists? Or just a series of snapshots, all equally real, spread across time?

4.3 Group discussion

One person in the group records the answers below. Everyone else: write that person's name on your sheet instead.
5
If the block universe is right, does the concept of personal identity even make sense? Or does identity require time to flow in one direction?
6
The Prestige copies you across space. Eternal Sunshine erases you across time. Arrival collapses time entirely. Which one poses the biggest threat to the idea that there's a stable "you" at all?
